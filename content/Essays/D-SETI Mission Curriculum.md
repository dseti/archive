## Topics

### Dreamwork practices

Dreamwork is the practice of working with dreams, which is an essential part of shamanic cultures.  All major religions have seen dreams as a domain of contact with spiritual forces, as a means to receive divine inspiration or become enlightened.  Dream shamanic cultures use dreams as source of knowledge, guidance, healing, empathy, insight, and much more.

While dreamwork is a universal human practice, cultural adoption varies.  For example, Western materialist culture does not honor dreams as real, therefore often rejects dreamwork altogether.  Modern understandings of dreamwork begin with Freud and his “discovery” of the unconscious. Psychoanalytic ideas have dominated the study and practice of dreamwork, although psychoanalysis is now understood as a tradition of practice and not a science per se.   Contemporary dream science is based on neuroscientific understanding of dreams, combined with content analysis of dream reports from studied populations.

The modern dreamwork movement in the West began mid-20th century with Kilton Stewart’s sensational reports of dreamwork among the Senoi, an indigenous group in Malaysia.  His romantic depiction of a utopian society based around dream sharing inspired a generation of dreamworkers to work with dreams in groups.  However, even skeptical scholars point out that Stewart’s description was more fantasy than fact, we must remember than dream shamanism is a universal human trait.  

D-SETI looks to the Ullman Dream Appreciation Method as a simple and effective way to work with dreams.

Key ideas

- Dreamwork is a universal human practice
- Dreamwork practices vary between cultures and groups
- Post-enlightment Western culture rejected dreams and dreamwork as unscientific
- Freud and the psychoanalysts reintroduced dreamwork to the Western mainstream
- Dream science is based on neuroscience and content analysis of dream reports
- Modern dreamwork derives from a cultural movement inspired by Stewart’s Dream Theory in Malaya

### Science of sleep and dreaming

Science is not the end-all-be-all for understanding dreams that it claims to be.  In fact, science has yet to explain why and how we dream.  However, science can provide key insights and frameworks for understanding many aspects of dreaming and can help us discern how dreams can be the real domain of ET/NHI contact.

Key ideas

- The science of sleep and dreaming is a relatively new domain of science
- Freud reintroduced dreaming and the unconscious to Western science, but psychoanalysis is no longer considered scientific
- Modern sleep and dream science is based on neuroscience and content analysis of dream reports
- There are many theories of sleeping and dreaming, but science has yet to explain why and how we dream
- Lucid dreaming is a scientifically proven phenomenon and may be used as an experimental method

### Multiplicity of dreams

There are many types of dreams that seem unreal or made up unless you’ve had them.  For example, only around 25% of people have had an out of body experience, which sound unbelievable to the unexperienced, but are often painfully real to those who experience them.  It’s helpful to know about all the different types of dreams you might encounter.

Key ideas

- There are many types of dreams, which is a core principle of dream shamanism
- Types of dreams include aspirational, noctural, nap, hypnagogic/hypnapopic, hypnotic, exotic, anomalous, noetic, transcendent, nightmare, etc.
- Common, yet under reported phenomenon include sleep paralysis, waking nightmare, hypnagogic/pompic hallucination, out of body, and visitation dreams
- Scientists have documented a spectrum of sleep and dream disorders that are relevant to DSETI

### Biological and psychological theories

There are many biological and psychological theories of dreaming.  It is important to familiarize yourself with the various sleep stages like rapid eye movement sleep, or REM, when most of our dreams occur.  Each field of science has its own theory of dreaming.  For example, the AIM model explains how dreams may arise from activations, inputs, and modulation of the brain’s biology, which is a neuroscientific theory.  In contrast, social scientists might theorize that dreaming simulates social situations or that dream sharing increases social cohesion.

Key ideas

- Biological theories of dreaming explore why the body dreams, such as for memory consolidation
- Biological theories make sense of sleep lab data such as EEG and dream logs
- Psychological theories involve why we dream such as to simulate situations for problem solving
- Psychological theories make sense of personal reports, interpretations of culture, etc
- Biological and psychological theories, by definition, are unable to explain spiritual or shamanistic aspects of dreaming, but may provide insight
- The hard sciences have documented many ET/NHI contact phenomenon most commonly as sleep paralysis and hypnagogic hallucinations

Dreams respond to your expectations and beliefs, therefore it is possible that other cultures experience different types of dreaming.  Many shamanic dreaming cultures understand that words have power, so they might not tell you their dreams, even if you are an anthropologist.  It is important to study historical and cross-cultural perspectives because they may reveal new worlds of dreaming.

Key ideas

# 3. Lucid Dreaming

## Overview

Lucid dreams are dreams in which you are aware you are dreaming, sometimes you can remember your daily life and  control the dream.  Lucid dreams are particularly interesting because they are a scientifically proven phenomenon and provide a powerful experimental method.  You can likely learn to lucid dream by finding a technique that works for you.

The popular conception of lucid dreaming suggests that it is the only peak experience of dreaming and that control implies mastery.  To the materialistic mind, a dream might either be a normal dream, in which case it is meaningless fantasy, or else it is a lucid dream, in which case it may have meaning for several reasons.

DSETI sees lucid dreaming as a useful tool for SETI experiments, but ultimately lucid dreaming is one of many types of dreaming relevant to the DSETI mission.  Lucid dreaming enables repeatable experiments that integrate subjective dreaming content, directed through intent, with objective sleep data (correlated through rapid eye movement). 

Key ideas

- Lucid dreams are dreams in which you are aware of dreaming
- Lucidity is a strategy, not type, of dreaming
- Lucid dreaming risks hypnagogic hallucinations and waking nightmares
- Early lucid dream experiments may explain UFO Abduction Syndrome narratives

- The DSETI Mission Training prepares you to participate in the DSETI lucid dreaming experiments

## Topics

### Definition

Lucid dreaming was defined in 1913 in an article, A Study of Dreams, in which Frederik van Eeden described his own experiences.  He had dreams in which he was a) aware he was dreaming, b) remembered his daily life, and c) could enact his intent.  Lucid dreaming was scientifically proven by Alan Worsely and Keith Hearn in Hearn’s PhD dissertation, which was later confirmed by Stephen LaBerge in his dissertation.  LaBerge popularized the concept of lucid dreaming that permeates mainstream culture.

Key ideas

- Lucid dreams are dreams in which you are aware
- People use lucid dream incubation techniques to induce lucid dreams
- MILD stands for mnemonic induced lucid dreams, which is the most approachable method
- WILD stands for wake induced lucid dreaming, which risks the hypnagogic hallucinations and waking nightmares essential to understanding the UFO Abduction Syndrome
- Lucid dreaming was scientifically proven by lucid dreamers who moved their eyes in a pre-determined pattern during REM sleep while hooked up to sleep monitors
- The first lucid dreamer to prove lucid dreaming also testified to a stage of dreaming that involved ultrareal scenarios akin to alien encounters
- People often over only familiar with lucid dreaming, therefore use the term to signify other types of dreaming such as noetic, precognitive, telepathic, out of body, hypnagogic, etc

After reading about lucid dreaming, it is easy to assume that other dreaming cultures care about lucid dreaming for the same reasons.  For example, one might assume that the Tibetan Yogas of Dream and Sleep is a mythic and unscientific understanding of lucid dreaming.  However, reducing everything to lucid dreaming often misses the point.  One can expand their perspective by cross-cultural practice and study, beginning with one’s own culture.  There are mystical traditions within every religion that deal with the interpretation and control of dream states.

Key ideas

- Lucid dreaming is a modern western concept
- Integral studies offers a framework for cross-cultural studies
- All religions have mystical traditions that involve the control and interpretation of dream and trance states, similar to lucid dream control
- Asclepian dream incubation is an example of dream incubation and 
- Tibetan Dream Yoga of Sleep and Dream is an accessible example lucid dreaming within a religious tradition
- Ethical guidelines recommend that dreamworkers are educated in cross-cultural and spiritual perspective of dreams through study that includes personal dreamwork practice

### WILD, hypnagogia, sleep paralysis, and entities

Lucid dreaming may explain UFO abduction and UAP disclosure narratives because it gives us experimental access to stages of sleep/dreams that involve ultra-real scenarios of visitation by strange beings.

Lucid dreams are interesting to scientists because they prove the correlation between subjective dream reports and objective measurements of the body.  Lucid dream training also enables dreamers to remember and conduct experimental instructions, such as move their eyes according to a predetermined pattern or to perform some action in a dream.
